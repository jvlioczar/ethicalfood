Reasons to consider cellular agriculture

Our own summary of 90 Reasons to Consider Cellular Agriculture by Kristopher Gasteratos (2019), with the original numbering.

How to read this. These are 2019 projections of the technology’s potential, not measured results, and the author accepts that some may not come true. Percentages compare estimates: later life cycle assessments show that impact depends heavily on energy source and scale. The list covers cellular agriculture, not plant-based proteins. See also Julio Cesar Prava’s opinion piece (in Portuguese).

Health and food safety

01

No pathogen contamination

Monitored production with little human handling should lower the risk of Salmonella or E. coli reaching food.

02

Lower famine risk

Food supply depends less on livestock and can continue through events that would otherwise stop harvests.

03

No fecal contamination

Most fecal contamination happens at slaughter. Removing slaughter removes that route; a 2015 Consumer Reports test found fecal-indicator bacteria in all beef sampled.

04

No added hormones

Growth hormones used to speed up animal growth would not be needed.

05

Longer shelf life

Cleaner production could keep food safe for longer.

06

Feeding the future

An added supply route for animal products as the population approaches 10 billion by 2050.

07

No swine or avian flu

Without farmed animals, this source of zoonotic outbreaks goes away.

08

Geographic independence

Regions with poor access to animal products could make them locally.

09

Natural disasters

Animals left behind in floods can contaminate water supplies, as happened after Hurricane Florence. No livestock, no such risk.

10

Heme iron without animals

People with iron-deficiency conditions who avoid animal products would gain another source of heme iron.

11

No botulism from carcasses

Not handling slaughtered animals reduces this route of infection.

12

Man-made disasters

Land-based production sidesteps contamination of seafood by events such as oil spills.

13

Safe food in a catastrophe

Secure cell banks could provide radiation-free food after a nuclear war or major impact.

14

No antibiotics in food

The process is not expected to rely on the antibiotics used in animal farming.

15

Fewer superbugs

Less antibiotic use by the animal protein sector means less pressure toward resistance.

16

Healthier foods

Nutrition could be tuned, for example with less fat and more protein.

17

No plastic in seafood

Fish that never swam in the sea do not ingest ocean plastic.

18

No mercury in seafood

Seafood made without catching fish avoids mercury accumulation.

19

Safer vegetables

Livestock runoff can contaminate nearby crops, as in a Romaine lettuce recall linked to a cattle farm.

20

Options for restricted diets

People who avoid animal products but are allergic to soy, nuts or gluten would have more choices.

21

Biomedical spillovers

Advances could carry over to regenerative medicine and other fields.

22

Whey without cows

Athletes who only use plant protein could have an animal-free whey.

23

Prion monitoring

Monitored systems can screen for prions, helping avoid outbreaks like mad cow disease.

24

Hypoallergenic milk

Dairy could be made suitable for people with milk allergies, especially children.

25

Better taste

Control over nutritional profiles could also improve flavor.

Environment: ecosystems

44

45% less energy

Estimated energy saving for meat. Later studies show this depends heavily on the energy source.

45

Less animal waste on land

No livestock means none of the 1.75 billion tons of manure that pollute groundwater.

46

Less animal waste at sea

Less farm runoff means fewer dead zones and algal blooms.

47

No bottom trawling

Seafood without nets dragged across the seabed, which destroys old coral.

48

No fish blood discharge

Aquaculture would no longer release virus-carrying blood into public waters.

49

Fewer pesticides

Less feed-crop runoff of pesticides and herbicides into oceans.

50

Less transport pollution

Local production can shorten land and sea freight.

51

Fewer fishing fleets

Local production reduces polluting fleets.

52

Less deforestation

Lower grain demand removes a major pressure on forests and leaves room to reforest.

53

A plastic alternative

Cell-grown rhino horn (keratin) could replace some plastics.

54

Less ocean plastic

Fishing gear is a large share of plastic in the Great Pacific Garbage Patch (46% in one study).

Human and animal rights

55

Safer jobs

Less exposure to the injury and amputation risks of industrial meat work.

56

Lower psychological harm

Workers avoid the trauma, including PTSD, linked to repeated slaughter.

57

Fewer recall costs

Fewer cullings and contamination recalls paid for with public money.

58

Safer than tanneries

Cell-grown leather avoids toxic tannery exposures linked to long-term illness.

59

Less seafood fraud

Tighter control reduces mislabeling, which one survey put at 1 in 3 fish.

60

No forced labor at sea

Production without fishing fleets removes the setting for forced labor in that industry.

61

Cheaper food

The source expects prices to keep falling. This remains unproven at scale.

62

An end to killing for food

Over a trillion animals, most of them fish, are killed for food each year. This removes the need.

63

Less animal suffering

An alternative to factory farming that needs no animals to be raised.

64

Less poaching

Cell-grown ivory or rhino horn may undercut the market for poached products.

65

Pet food

An alternative for owners who do not want to feed their pets conventional animal products.

66

Less overfishing

Seafood demand can be met without taking fish from threatened ecosystems.

67

No bycatch

No nets means no dolphins and turtles caught by accident.

68

Protecting endangered species

Species targeted directly, such as sharks for fin soup, face less pressure.

69

Less biodiversity loss

Less farmland expansion means less habitat loss.

70

Grain for people

Grain from poorer countries would no longer be shipped to feed factory-farmed animals.

71

Environmental justice

Low-income communities near livestock operations would be spared manure spraying and its health effects.

72

Less wildlife hunting

Products such as bear gallbladder could be made without hunting.

Business and economics

73

Financial security

Fewer shocks from food scandals, disasters and costly recalls.

74

Carbon tax exposure

Lower-impact products would be less exposed to future carbon pricing.

75

Product consistency

Tighter process control gives more consistent output and less fraud.

76

Welfare premium

Consumers who pay more for high-welfare products may pay for this too.

77

Independent of climate

Production can run in hot or cold regions alike.

78

Property values

Homes near intensive livestock facilities lose value, by up to 88% at very close range, per the sources cited.

79

No surplus buying

Less need for public purchases of surplus, such as the US$20 million of US cheese in 2016.

80

Domestic production

Land-constrained nations such as Singapore and Japan could produce at home.

81

Byproduct supply

By-products such as horn could also be grown if farmed supply falls.

82

Fewer bailouts

Less need for government safety nets for dairy farmers.

83

New jobs

A new industry creates biotech and agricultural jobs.

84

Less dairy waste

Better control of output means less wasted dairy.

Future and beyond

85

Food on Mars

A more sustainable way to feed long missions and future settlements.

86

Personal production

Home devices could one day let people make custom products.

87

New animal foods

A wider range of products, including species no longer farmed or even extinct.

88

Astronaut health

Diet could be adjusted to conditions such as muscle atrophy in space.

89

Food safety in space

Tightly controlled environments lower pathogen exposure on missions.

90

New materials

Biomaterials could offer strength and toughness for demanding uses.
